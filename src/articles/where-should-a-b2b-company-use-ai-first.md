Most owners I meet are asking the same thing right now: where should we use AI first?

I spent nine years on the other side of that question. I started Upstack Studio in 2017 and ran it until 2026. Companies came to me with software they wanted built. I wrote the proposals. I delivered the projects.

Almost nobody brought a number. Nobody could tell me what the problem was costing them that month. So nobody could tell me whether the thing they wanted was worth building.

That's the trouble with "where should we use AI?" It sounds like a technology question. It isn't. It's a money question, and you can answer it with figures you already have.

I closed Upstack in 2026. The agency kept earning. It just wasn't the model I wanted to run anymore, because the overhead was fixed and I couldn't grow it without adding more people. I held on longer than I should have, and that cost me about RM600,000 of my own money.

So I'm not neutral here. I've been the vendor writing the proposal. I've also been the owner who did the maths on his own business too late.

Here's the method I use now. You can run it yourself in about two hours.

## Step 1: Stop asking "how can we use AI?"

Every business can use AI somewhere, so that question always produces a list. Twenty ideas, all possible, nothing to tell you which one to fund.

You can't act on a list like that. You have one budget and one team's attention this quarter. You need to know which single item deserves them.

Ask this instead:

**Where is the business losing money, repeating work, or deciding without enough information?**

Answer that first. Then ask whether AI changes anything about it.

Some of your best answers won't involve AI at all. That's the method working, not failing.

## Step 2: Find the leak, and pull the actual numbers

Look in three places. Give yourself a week and pull real figures for each one. The rest of the method is worthless without them.

**Money leaking after you already paid to get it.** You spent the marketing budget. The lead arrived. Then something went wrong. Pull these:

- Quotes you sent last quarter, and how many closed
- Days between an enquiry arriving and your first human reply
- Customers who ordered in the previous 12 months but not in the last 6
- Your top 20 accounts by revenue, and when each one last bought

**Work that repeats and needs no judgment.** Pull these:

- The same data typed into two systems. Count records a week, times minutes each
- Reports rebuilt by hand every week. Count hours per month
- Questions your team answers by email again and again. Count two weeks' worth
- Multiply those hours by a loaded hourly cost. Loaded means salary plus tax, benefits and the desk they sit at, not salary divided by 2,080

**Decisions you make blind.** These are hard to price, so write the question instead of a number:

- Which products or services actually make money after delivery cost?
- Which salesperson is doing badly, and how would you know?
- What does it cost you when stock is too high, or too low?

Pick the leak that's costing the most. Not the one that's most interesting to fix.

## Step 3: Sort what you found into three piles

Do this before anyone proposes a fix. The figures below are invented to show the shape of it. They're not from a real company.

| Pile | What goes in it | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Facts | Things you can pull from a system today | 412 quotes issued last quarter, 96 closed |
| Fair guesses | What the facts point to, but don't prove | Slow follow-up is part of why the rest went quiet |
| Unknowns | Things you'd have to measure to know | How many of the 316 were still open when we stopped replying |

Most bad AI projects start when a guess gets treated as a fact. Someone says follow-up is the problem. Everyone nods. Nobody checks. Six months later you own a tool nobody uses.

If your biggest unknown is cheap to measure, measure it before you buy anything. A week of counting beats a quarter of building.

## Step 4: Write down all five options

For the leak you picked, you have five honest options. AI is the right answer in some of them and the wrong answer in others. Two of them need no software at all.

1. **Fix the process.** Change who does what, when, and in what order. No software.
2. **Buy something that exists.** Someone has probably solved this. Pay them.
3. **Automate the part that repeats.** Rules, links between your systems, and AI for steps that repeat and follow rules.
4. **Build something custom.** Only when the job is unique to you and worth paying to maintain for years.
5. **Do nothing.** The leak is real but small, and your attention is worth more elsewhere.

Write all five down every time, including the ones you don't like. A list with one item on it isn't a decision.

One warning on options 3 and 4. AI is good at work that repeats and follows rules. It can't replace the person who holds the customer relationship. It can't make the judgment call about what the business does next. Give the machine the repeats and keep your people on the judgment.

## Step 5: Score all five options

Score each option from 0 to 5 on the five tests below, then add up the row. Zero means it changes nothing at all.

| Test | The question it answers |
| --- | --- |
| Money | If this works, what changes in ringgit or dollars? |
| Proof | How much of that number is fact rather than hope? |
| Speed | Weeks or quarters before anything is different? |
| Fit | Does this matter to where the business is going? |
| Ease | Can your actual team run this once it exists? |

Here's a worked example. The company's leak is quotes nobody chased. The scores are made up to show how the grid behaves.

| Option | Money | Proof | Speed | Fit | Ease | Total |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Fix the process | 4 | 4 | 5 | 3 | 4 | 20 |
| Buy a CRM add-on | 4 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 3 | 17 |
| Automate follow-up with AI | 5 | 3 | 3 | 4 | 3 | 18 |
| Build custom | 5 | 2 | 1 | 3 | 1 | 12 |
| Do nothing | 0 | 5 | 5 | 1 | 5 | 16 |

The grid rarely picks the winner by itself. Its real job is to show you where you disagree. When two people score Money 5 and 2, you've found the argument worth having, and it's almost always about proof rather than about AI.

Watch any option that scores high on Money and low on Proof. That pair is how companies spend real money on a guess.

## Step 6: Choose, then write down why

- The highest total with real proof behind it wins.
- If the top two are close, take the faster one. You'll learn more from finishing.
- If nothing clears the bar, do nothing and say so out loud. "Not yet" is a real answer.

Then write one paragraph. What you chose. What you turned down. What would have to change to bring a rejected option back. In six months, when someone asks why you're not doing the other thing, that paragraph is your answer.

## Step 7: Turn the winner into a small, timed test

Give it edges. Fill in these five lines before anyone starts work:

- **The job:** the leak you picked.
- **The owner:** a named person, not a committee.
- **The number:** what you expect to move, measured before you start.
- **The window:** 90 days. Long enough to be real, short enough to stop.
- **The stop rule:** what result makes you kill it, decided now while you're still calm.

Measure the starting number first. If you can't measure it, you can't judge the result. Go back to step 2 and pick something you can count.

## Step 8: Six questions before you approve any spend

Make whoever is proposing the work answer these. Internal team or outside vendor, same list.

1. Which business problem does this address, and what is it costing us now?
2. Which parts of that number are measured, and which are guessed?
3. What are the other four options, and why did this one win?
4. What has to be true for this to work?
5. How will we know in 90 days whether it worked?
6. What happens to this if you disappear?

I wrote proposals for nine years. A proposal that can't answer those six questions isn't a plan for your business. It's a plan for someone else's revenue, and I say that as someone who used to sit on the other end of it.
